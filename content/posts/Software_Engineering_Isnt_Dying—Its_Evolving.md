title: "Software Engineering Isn't Dying—It's Evolving"

AI is enabling software engineers to move up the value chain. This realization should be more obvious than it is. The AI era feels different from prior technology-driven disruptions because it is fundamentally different. This is not analogous to the shift from horse-drawn carriages to automobiles. While there were undoubtedly those who took pride in crafting carriages—and the Luddites of England who resisted the mechanized loom that produced more in a day than a worker could in a week—this transformation is distinct because it affects everyone, directly or indirectly. It is unsettling to the broader public as they read about once-elite, untouchable software engineers facing their own reckoning, leading many to wonder whether they, too, are at risk. Those really smart people can't get jobs, what about me? That, however, is a matter of perception rather than reality.

 Great software engineers excel at abstracting problems, writing code, and optimizing performance. These skills are not replaced; they are redeployed. Software engineers must embrace the future now that the AI genie is out of the bottle. AI is not going away. The software engineering community has navigated such transitions before: from rewiring boards to flipping switches, from writing assembly on punch cards to COBOL and Fortran, from coding on a terminal to the current evolution of AI. Each step was predicted to reduce the need for software engineers; the opposite occurred every time. Jevons' paradox has held true for every evolutionary advancement in software engineering. Today, media pundits claim that this time is different. Is it? Why do we believe that? Should we? The pundit class has proven to be an unreliable predictor of the future. I am not convinced. Fear sells; optimism does not.

Software engineering as a whole is not going anywhere. The tools we use today will soon be replaced by better ones. The tools of the future will not be coded solely by engineers. They will be developed by engineers augmented by AI coding tools. Software engineers will still receive user specifications that must be translated into prompts, and hallucinations will need to be debugged, either by refactoring prompts or by engineers fixing buggy code. Software engineers will write less code but produce more. This means tackling more problems, not fewer. The role and value of software engineers will not diminish; it will expand and increase. In fact, senior engineers with leadership experience are well-positioned to capitalize on emerging AI opportunities. If you can delegate to people, you can delegate to AI agents. Eventually, even managing multiple agents will reach its limits, as a single engineer can only manage so many. We simply do not yet know what those limits are. Even without experience delegating to employees, software engineers are accustomed to functional decomposition. When it comes to managing agents, the problem shares the same structure as delegating work to people. If you can break a problem down, that is the basis for an agent, greatly oversimplified, I acknowledge that.

The remaining challenge is training junior engineers and determining where to place them. We will always need more senior engineers, so shutting down the pipeline of entry-level talent will only work for so long before a shortage of people becomes the problem. I expect a shortage of senior-level software engineers in a year or two. The hype that AI will destroy the labor market will blow over. So relax, time is on your side, eventually the AI washing and the over-hiring from COVID will have run its course. Just don't relax too much, build up your AI coding skills!
